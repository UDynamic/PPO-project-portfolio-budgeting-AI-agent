\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:overview}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

This section develops the formal problem structure for portfolio-level budget
allocation in contracted project portfolios. The formulation proceeds in three
levels of increasing generality, each building directly on the preceding one.

\textbf{Level~1 — Deterministic Full-Foresight Linear Program.}
All contractor performance parameters, expenditure trajectories, and payment
timing are treated as known with certainty. The resulting program is a linear
program (LP) in the continuous budget allocation variables. Because no real
decision-maker possesses this information ex ante, the Level~1 optimum
constitutes a theoretical upper bound on portfolio performance: no
implementable policy can exceed it. This upper bound anchors the empirical
comparison in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\textbf{Level~2 — Stochastic Extension and Intractability.}
Deterministic performance parameters are replaced by stochastic processes
consistent with the empirical EVM literature. The resulting multi-stage
stochastic program is formally characterized, and the combinatorial growth of
its scenario tree with the number of projects and planning periods is
established. This intractability argument motivates the reformulation in
Level~3.

\textbf{Level~3 — Constrained Markov Decision Process.}
The stochastic program of Level~2 is reformulated as a Constrained Markov
Decision Process (CMDP). An explicit mapping is provided from LP elements to
MDP primitives: decision variables map to actions, constraint parameters map to
state variables, the objective maps to the reward function, and feasibility
constraints define the constrained action space. The CMDP is implemented as a
configurable, Gymnasium-compatible synthetic environment; a Proximal Policy
Optimization (PPO) agent trained on this environment serves as the primary
computational demonstration of the framework's utility.

The three levels are not alternative models of the same problem. They are a
coherent modeling arc: Level~1 defines the problem's theoretical ceiling,
Level~2 characterizes why that ceiling is computationally unreachable, and
Level~3 provides the tractable reformulation that makes the problem solvable at
realistic scale. The remainder of this section develops each level in turn,
preceded by the shared problem primitives — project and portfolio definitions,
contractual cash-flow mechanics, and S-curve expenditure dynamics — on which
all three levels draw.

The formulation integrates domain knowledge and established methodologies from
project management and operations research: S-curve expenditure dynamics
\cite{kenley1986relationship,barraza2004probabilistic}, Earned Value Management
(EVM) performance tracking \cite{fleming2016earned}, milestone-based payment
structures consistent with FIDIC contract forms
\cite{fidic2017silver,cidb2015construction,eggers2019lump}, performance
recovery interventions \cite{merrow2011industrial}, and the theory of
investment under uncertainty as it applies to abandonment options
\cite{dixit1994investment}. The LP of Level~1 is situated within the
stochastic programming tradition \cite{birge2011introduction}; the CMDP of
Level~3 is situated within the constrained MDP literature
\cite{altman1999constrained}, framed as a direct descendant of Bellman's
dynamic programming and stochastic control theory rather than as an import from
machine learning.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Scope and Positioning}
\label{subsec:scope}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The problem addressed here is operational, not strategic. The literature on
project portfolio management has devoted considerable attention to the
\emph{selection} problem: given a candidate set of projects and a capital
budget, which subset should the organization commit to
\cite{archer1999project,kavadias2003resource}? This work takes the selection
decision as given. A portfolio of contracted projects exists; commitments have
been signed; and the relevant decision-maker — a portfolio manager or governing
board — faces a recurring operational question: how should the available budget
be allocated across active projects at each discrete planning period?

This is the budget allocation control problem, and it is the sole focus of the
formulation. The decision-maker exercises one lever: the amount of budget
directed to each active project at each period. All strategic and operational
outcomes of interest — contractor performance recovery, milestone achievement,
cash flow balance, delay penalty exposure, and ultimate project success or
termination — flow through this single allocation decision. The formulation
makes this causal chain explicit and embeds it within a sequential
decision-making framework.

The model is deliberately general. Every structural element — the number of
projects, the milestone structure, the S-curve shape parameters, the advance
payment and retention ratios, the performance response distribution, the
intervention threshold and cost, the termination settlement mechanics — is
parameterized rather than fixed. Sector-specific instantiation is achieved
through empirical calibration of these parameters, not through changes to the
model structure. The calibration used in this work draws on anonymized data
from two major EPC contractors, supplemented by World Bank ICR data
\cite{worldbank2023icr} and infrastructure cost overrun datasets
\cite{flyvbjerg2003underestimating}. The general structure is applicable across
construction, software development, defense contracting, and other
project-intensive sectors \cite{kenley1986relationship,christensen1993empirical}.

One deliberate boundary requires explicit statement. The model represents the
financial relationship between a single client organization (the portfolio
owner) and its contracted project delivery partners (the contractors). The
client allocates budget; the contractor expends it and delivers progress. No
third-party management consultant, program management contractor, or
intermediary role is modeled. This two-party structure is the minimal
representation consistent with the contractual mechanics encoded in the
formulation and is retained across all three levels. Extensions to multi-party
governance structures are noted as a direction for future work.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Fundamental Modeling Choices}
\label{subsec:fundamental_designs}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Four structural choices underlie the entire formulation. Each is stated here
with its justification, because they determine the form of every constraint and
variable that follows.

\paragraph{A portfolio is an aggregation of independent bilateral contracts.}
Each project in the portfolio corresponds to a separate contract between the
client and a distinct contractor. Projects do not share resources, workforces,
or contractual obligations within the formulation; interaction occurs only
through the shared portfolio budget constraint. This independence structure is
consistent with standard EPC contracting practice and permits the portfolio
problem to be decomposed into project-level dynamics aggregated at the portfolio
level. It is also the structure that makes the LP of Level~1 tractable: the
coupling across projects enters only through the single budget constraint at
each period.

\paragraph{The resource allocation problem reduces to budget allocation.}
Within each project, the contractor controls workforce, materials, equipment,
and subcontracting. From the portfolio manager's perspective, the only
instrument available is the budget directed to each project. The contractor's
internal response to that budget — the efficiency with which it is converted
into physical progress — is modeled as a performance parameter, stochastic at
Level~2 and treated as known at Level~1. This reduction of the resource
allocation problem to a scalar budget allocation per project per period is both
a modeling choice and a reflection of the actual decision authority of a
portfolio-level governing body.

\paragraph{Time is discrete, with periods representing calendar months.}
The planning horizon $\mathcal{T} = \{1, \ldots, H\}$ is indexed by discrete
periods. Monthly periods are adopted because the key governance events in
portfolio management — progress reporting, payment certification, performance
review meetings — are conventionally held on a monthly cycle. The LP of
Level~1 optimizes over the full horizon simultaneously; the CMDP of Level~3
operates period by period, with decisions made sequentially as new information
is revealed.

\paragraph{Contract mechanics are calibrated to FIDIC forms.}
The milestone-based payment structure, advance payment and recovery mechanism,
retention and release, delay penalty with cap, and termination settlement
mechanics are all specified to be consistent with FIDIC Red, Yellow, and Silver
Book contract forms \cite{fidic2017silver}. This anchors the formulation in
the dominant international EPC contracting framework and ensures that the model
parameters have direct empirical counterparts in contractor financial data.
Where FIDIC allows variation by agreement (e.g., the advance payment ratio, the
retention percentage, the delay penalty rate), these are treated as
project-specific calibrated parameters rather than fixed constants.

\subsection{Problem Primitives}
\label{subsec:primitives}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

This subsection establishes the shared notation, project-level parameters,
contractual cash-flow mechanics, and portfolio aggregation structure on which
all three levels of the formulation draw. Nothing introduced here is
level-specific; each symbol defined below appears, without redefinition, in
the LP of Level~1, the stochastic extension of Level~2, and the CMDP of
Level~3.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Sets and Indices}
\label{subsubsec:sets}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

\begin{description}
  \item[$\mathcal{P} = \{1, \ldots, n\}$]
    Set of projects in the portfolio, indexed by $i$. All projects are
    assumed committed at the start of the planning horizon; no new projects
    enter mid-horizon.

  \item[$\mathcal{T} = \{1, \ldots, H\}$]
    Set of discrete planning periods, indexed by $t$, each representing one
    calendar month.

  \item[$\mathcal{M}_i = \{1, \ldots, M_i\}$]
    Ordered set of contractual milestones for project $i$, indexed by $j$.
    Milestones are ordered by their progress thresholds:
    $\theta_{i,1} < \theta_{i,2} < \cdots < \theta_{i,M_i} = 1$.

  \item[$\mathcal{A}_i^{\mathrm{plan}} = \{t : s_i \leq t \leq f_i\}$]
    Planned execution window of project $i$, determined by the planned start
    $s_i$ and planned finish $f_i$.

  \item[$\mathcal{P}_t^{\mathrm{active}} \subseteq \mathcal{P}$]
    Subset of projects active at period $t$. In Level~1 (full foresight),
    this set is determined ex ante from the known efficiency trajectories
    $\{\bar{\eta}_{i,t}\}$ and is treated as a fixed parameter. In
    Level~2 and Level~3, it is endogenous, depending on realized project
    status transitions.

  \item[$\mathcal{M}_i^{\mathrm{cert}}(t) \subseteq \mathcal{M}_i$]
    Subset of milestones of project $i$ certified up to and including period
    $t$: $\mathcal{M}_i^{\mathrm{cert}}(t) = \{j \in \mathcal{M}_i :
    \theta_{i,j} \leq P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}}\}$.
\end{description}

\subsubsection{Planned Expenditure Profile (S-Curve)}
\label{subsubsec:scurve}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

The planned cumulative expenditure of project $i$ follows a normalized S-curve
\cite{kenley1986relationship, barraza2004probabilistic}. Define the normalized
planned time at period $t$ as:
\[
  \tau_{i,t} = \frac{t - s_i + 1}{D_i^{\mathrm{plan}}},
  \qquad t \in \mathcal{A}_i^{\mathrm{plan}},
\]
with truncation $\tau_{i,t}^{c} = \min\{1, \max\{0, \tau_{i,t}\}\}$ to handle
periods outside the planned window. The planned cumulative expenditure fraction
is:
\[
  F_i(\tau) = I_{\tau}(a_i, b_i),
\]
where $I_{\tau}(\cdot, \cdot)$ is the regularized incomplete Beta function and
$a_i, b_i > 0$ are empirically calibrated shape parameters. The planned
cumulative expenditure and planned period expenditure are then:
\[
  \bar{E}_{i,t} = \mathrm{BAC}_i \cdot F_i(\tau_{i,t}^{c}),
  \qquad
  \Delta\bar{E}_{i,t} = \bar{E}_{i,t} - \bar{E}_{i,t-1}.
\]
The planned cumulative progress is assumed to track planned expenditure:
$P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{plan}} = F_i(\tau_{i,t}^{c})$.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{State Variables}
\label{subsubsec:state_variables}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

At each period $t$, the state of project $i$ is characterized by the
following variables. In Level~1 these are deterministic functions of the
allocation decisions $\{x_{i,t}\}$ under known efficiency parameters. In
Level~2 and Level~3 they are stochastic.

\begin{description}
  \item[\ensuremath{P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}} \in [0,1]}]
    Cumulative actual progress of project $i$ at the end of period $t$:
    the fraction of project scope physically completed.

  \item[\ensuremath{E_{i,t} \geq 0}]
    Cumulative actual expenditure on project $i$ up to and including
    period $t$.

  \item[\ensuremath{\mathrm{SPI}_{i,t}}]
    Schedule Performance Index at period $t$:
    \[
      \mathrm{SPI}_{i,t}
        = \frac{P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}}}{P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{plan}}},
      \qquad P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{plan}} > 0.
    \]
    Values below unity indicate schedule underperformance.

  \item[\ensuremath{\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}}]
    Cost Performance Index at period $t$:
    \[
      \mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}
        = \frac{P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}} \cdot \mathrm{BAC}_i}{E_{i,t}},
      \qquad E_{i,t} > 0.
    \]
    Values below unity indicate cost overrun relative to work performed.

  \item[\ensuremath{B_t \geq 0}]
    Available portfolio cash balance at the beginning of period $t$.

  \item[\ensuremath{I_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}}]
    Intervention indicator: $I_{i,t} = 1$ if a corrective intervention
    is triggered for project $i$ at period $t$, else $0$. Determined by
    the environment as a function of the schedule delay
    $\Delta_{i,t} = P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{plan}} - P_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{actual}}$
    relative to the tolerance threshold $\tau_i$.

  \item[\ensuremath{\mathrm{status}_{i,t} \in \{\mathrm{active},\,
    \mathrm{completed},\, \mathrm{terminated}\}}]
    Categorical project status at period $t$. Transitions are governed by
    the progress recursion and the termination conditions defined below.
\end{description}

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Budget-to-Progress Conversion}
\label{subsubsec:budget_to_progress}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

The decision variable $x_{i,t} \geq 0$ is the budget allocated to project $i$
at period $t$. Allocated budget is converted to progress increment through an
efficiency parameter:

\[
  \Delta P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}}
    = \eta_{i,t} \cdot \frac{x_{i,t}}{\mathrm{BAC}_i},
\]

where $\eta_{i,t}$ is the \emph{budget-to-progress efficiency parameter}
for project $i$ at period $t$. This parameter captures all sources of
conversion uncertainty — workforce productivity, supply chain conditions,
technical difficulty, weather — that determine how much physical progress a
given budget expenditure yields.

\paragraph{Treatment across levels.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Level~1 (deterministic):} $\eta_{i,t}$ is replaced by a
    known deterministic value $\bar{\eta}_{i,t}$, calibrated from the
    project's planned performance profile. Under the nominal plan,
    $\bar{\eta}_{i,t} = 1$ implies that budget expenditure tracks the
    S-curve exactly; deviations encode the known performance trajectory.

  \item \textbf{Level~2 and Level~3 (stochastic):} $\eta_{i,t}$ is a
    random primitive, realized only after $x_{i,t}$ is committed. Its
    distribution is calibrated from empirical EVM data; the distributional
    form is specified in the Methodology section.
\end{itemize}

The cumulative state recursion follows directly:
\[
  P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}}
    = P_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{actual}}
      + \eta_{i,t} \cdot \frac{x_{i,t}}{\mathrm{BAC}_i},
  \qquad
  E_{i,t} = E_{i,t-1} + x_{i,t},
\]
with $P_{i,0}^{\mathrm{actual}} = 0$ and $E_{i,0} = 0$.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Contractual Cash-Flow Mechanics}
\label{subsubsec:cashflow}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

Project cash flows consist of outflows (budget allocations $x_{i,t}$) and
inflows (contractual payments from the client). The payment structure is
consistent with FIDIC contract forms \cite{fidic2017silver} and comprises
three mechanisms: advance payment with progressive recovery, interim
milestone-based payments with retention, and final payment with retention
release.

\paragraph{Advance Payment.}
At project initiation (period $s_i$), the client disburses an advance
payment:
\[
  A_i = \alpha_i \cdot CP_i.
\]
The advance payment is a contractual timing adjustment, not a financing
instrument: it carries no interest obligation and is not recorded as debt
\cite{cidb2015construction, eggers2019lump}. It is secured by an Advance
Payment Guarantee and recovered progressively through deductions from
subsequent milestone payments.

\paragraph{Advance Recovery.}
The recovery deduction applied at milestone $j$ is proportional to the
milestone's payment weight:
\[
  \Delta A_{i,j} = \phi_{i,j} \cdot A_i.
\]
This ensures that the full advance is recovered by the time all milestones
are certified, regardless of project duration.

\paragraph{Milestone Certification and Net Payment.}
Milestone $j \in \mathcal{M}_i$ is certified at the first period $t$ such
that $P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}} \geq \theta_{i,j}$. On certification, the
gross milestone payment, net payment after advance recovery, and retention
deduction are:
\[
  R_{i,j} = \phi_{i,j} \cdot CP_i \cdot (1 - \rho_i),
  \qquad
  R_{i,j}^{\mathrm{net}} = R_{i,j} - \Delta A_{i,j}.
\]
Advance payment recovery and retention operate independently: recovery
deductions are computed on gross payments prior to retention, and retained
amounts accumulate separately until released at completion. The two
mechanisms do not interact.

The milestone weights satisfy $\sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}_i} \phi_{i,j} = 1$,
so the sum of gross milestone payments equals $CP_i \cdot (1 - \rho_i)$
(i.e., the contract price net of total retained amount). The full accounting
identity across all payments is:
\[
  A_i + \sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}_i} R_{i,j}^{\mathrm{net}} + R_i^{\mathrm{ret}}
    = CP_i,
\]
where $R_i^{\mathrm{ret}} = \rho_i \cdot CP_i$ is the total retention
released at completion.

\paragraph{Final Payment and Retention Release.}
Upon project completion ($P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}} = 1$), the retained
amount is released and the delay penalty is applied. Let
$t_i^{\mathrm{actual}}$ denote the actual completion period and $f_i$ the
planned finish. The contractor-responsible delay is:
\[
  \Delta t_i^{\mathrm{contractor}}
    = \min\!\left\{
        0.5 \cdot \max\!\left\{0,\,
          \Delta t_i - \Delta t_i^{\mathrm{client}}\right\},\;
        \frac{D_i^{\mathrm{cap}}}{d_i}
      \right\},
  \qquad
  \Delta t_i = \max\{0,\, t_i^{\mathrm{actual}} - f_i\}.
\]
The convention that the contractor bears at most 50\% of the net delay
(after client-caused delay is deducted) reflects standard EPC industry
practice \cite{bryde2006project, flyvbjerg2003underestimating}. The delay
penalty and net final payment are:
\[
  DP_i = d_i \cdot \Delta t_i^{\mathrm{contractor}} \cdot CP_i,
  \qquad
  R_i^{\mathrm{final}}
    = R_{i,M_i}^{\mathrm{net}} + R_i^{\mathrm{ret}} - DP_i.
\]

\paragraph{Environment-Driven Dynamics: Intervention and Termination.}
Two additional mechanisms govern project trajectories and generate cash-flow
consequences, but are not decision variables — they are environment-driven
state transitions. Their financial effects enter the cash-flow mechanics and
constraints; their triggers are determined by the environment as a function
of the realized project state.

\emph{Corrective intervention.} When the schedule shortfall exceeds the
tolerance threshold,
\[
  \Delta_{i,t} = P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{plan}} - P_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{actual}}
    > \tau_i,
\]
the environment triggers a one-time corrective intervention at period $t$,
incurring cost $\chi_i$ and imposing a minimum allocation floor in the
subsequent period. The intervention indicator is:
\[
  I_{i,t} = \mathbf{1}\!\left\{\Delta_{i,t} > \tau_i\right\}.
\]
If performance does not recover after the intervention, no further
intervention is triggered; the project proceeds toward potential
termination. This single-attempt design reflects empirical evidence on
diminishing returns from repeated interventions \cite{merrow2011industrial}.

\emph{Project termination.} A project is terminated at the first period $t$
at which all three of the following conditions hold simultaneously:
(i)~the expected remaining delay at completion exceeds the penalty cap
horizon ($\mathbb{E}[\Delta t_i \mid \mathcal{F}_t] > D_i^{\mathrm{cap}} /
d_i$); (ii)~an intervention was triggered at some prior period but
performance has not recovered; and (iii)~the decision-maker has sustained
minimal allocation to the project for $k$ consecutive periods. The
simultaneous satisfaction of all three conditions ensures that termination
occurs only when the project is both contractually disadvantageous and
operationally non-viable.

\emph{Termination settlement.} At the termination period $t_i^{\mathrm{term}}$,
the net settlement payment is:
\[
  V_i^{\mathrm{term}}
    = P_{i,t_i^{\mathrm{term}}}^{\mathrm{actual}} \cdot CP_i,
  \qquad
  A_i^{\mathrm{outstanding}}
    = A_i - \sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}_i^{\mathrm{cert}}(t_i^{\mathrm{term}})}
        \Delta A_{i,j},
  \qquad
  R_i^{\mathrm{term}}
    = V_i^{\mathrm{term}}(1-\rho_i) - A_i^{\mathrm{outstanding}}.
\]
No delay penalty applies at termination. If $R_i^{\mathrm{term}} < 0$,
the outstanding advance payment balance constitutes a net obligation from
the contractor to the client; available portfolio cash inflows are directed
to settle this obligation ahead of other commitments, consistent with FIDIC
termination clauses \cite{fidic2017silver}.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Portfolio Aggregation}
\label{subsubsec:portfolio_aggregation}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

The portfolio $\mathcal{P}$ aggregates $n$ projects whose individual
dynamics are coupled through a single shared resource: the portfolio cash
balance $B_t$. The portfolio-level cash balance evolves as:
\[
  B_{t+1}
    = B_t
      - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}_t^{\mathrm{active}}} x_{i,t}
      + V_t
      - C_t^{\mathrm{intervention}},
  \qquad \forall\, t \in \mathcal{T},
\]
where:
\begin{align*}
  V_t
    &= \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}}\; \sum_{j \in \mathcal{M}_i}
        R_{i,j}^{\mathrm{net}}
        \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\text{milestone } (i,j) \text{ newly certified at } t\}, \\
  C_t^{\mathrm{intervention}}
    &= \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}} \chi_i \cdot I_{i,t}.
\end{align*}
The balance $B_t \geq 0$ is enforced at all periods; no borrowing or credit
facility is available. This hard constraint eliminates the soft budget
dynamics present in formulations that allow internal credit and is
consistent with the governance structure of a portfolio-level board
operating under a fixed capital commitment \cite{kavadias2003resource}.

The portfolio state at period $t$ is the vector:
\[
  S_t
    = \bigl(
        \{P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}},\, E_{i,t},\,
          \mathrm{SPI}_{i,t},\, \mathrm{CPI}_{i,t},\,
          I_{i,t},\, \mathrm{status}_{i,t}\}_{i \in \mathcal{P}},\;
        B_t
      \bigr).
\]
The dimension of $S_t$ scales linearly with the number of projects $n$.
This linear scaling, combined with the full-horizon coupling through $B_t$,
characterizes the computational complexity of the portfolio allocation
problem and motivates the intractability argument in Level~2.

% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -
\subsubsection{Objective}
\label{subsubsec:objective}
% - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - -

The decision-maker seeks to maximize the discounted cumulative net cash flow
over the planning horizon:
\[
  \max_{\{x_{i,t}\}}
    \quad
    \mathbb{E}\!\left[
      \sum_{t=1}^{H} \gamma^{t-1}
        \!\left(
          V_t
          - \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}_t^{\mathrm{active}}} x_{i,t}
          - C_t^{\mathrm{intervention}}
          - C_t^{\mathrm{delay}}
        \right)
      + \gamma^{H} \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}} R_i^{\mathrm{final}}
    \right],
\]
where $C_t^{\mathrm{delay}}$ is the imputed portfolio-level delay cost —
a weighted sum of project-level schedule shortfalls representing
reputational, opportunity, or contractual exposure not captured by explicit
delay penalties. The expectation $\mathbb{E}[\cdot]$ is taken over the joint
distribution of $\{\eta_{i,t}\}$ and all other stochastic primitives. In
Level~1, the expectation collapses to a deterministic evaluation under
$\bar{\eta}_{i,t}$; the stochastic operator is retained in this shared
statement to preserve continuity across all three levels.

The constraints on $\{x_{i,t}\}$ are:
\[
  x_{i,t} \geq m_{i,t}
    = m_{i,t}^{0} + \chi_i \cdot I_{i,t-1},
  \qquad \forall\, i \in \mathcal{P}_t^{\mathrm{active}},\; t \in \mathcal{T},
\]
\[
  \sum_{i \in \mathcal{P}_t^{\mathrm{active}}} x_{i,t} \leq B_t,
  \qquad \forall\, t \in \mathcal{T},
\]
\[
  x_{i,t} \geq 0,
  \qquad \forall\, i \in \mathcal{P},\; t \in \mathcal{T}.
\]

The mandatory minimum $m_{i,t}$ has two components. The baseline minimum
$m_{i,t}^{0}$ reflects the contractual floor below which a project cannot
be maintained without triggering a default event; it is calibrated from
FIDIC minimum-progress obligations. The intervention term
$\chi_i \cdot I_{i,t-1}$ imposes an additional allocation floor in the
period immediately following an environment-triggered intervention, encoding
the resource commitment required to execute the corrective action.

These primitives — parameters, state variables, cash-flow mechanics, and
the shared objective statement — are the foundation from which each of the
three formulation levels is constructed. Level~1 specializes them under
deterministic full foresight; Level~2 reintroduces stochasticity explicitly;
Level~3 reformulates the resulting stochastic program as a CMDP.
