\subsection{Management Problem and Portfolio Setting}
\label{subsec:setting}

We consider a multi‑period budget allocation problem for a fixed portfolio of projects. The decision maker does not select which projects to undertake; the set of projects is given. Over a planning horizon \(t = 1,\dots,H\), the manager must distribute a limited budget among the active projects, monitor their progress, and decide when to apply corrective interventions. Each project consumes resources gradually, earns revenue through milestone payments, and is exposed to uncertainty in schedule, cost, and funding. The objective is to maximize a composite measure of portfolio value that rewards profitable completion, penalises excessive risk, and discourages wasteful spending on projects with poor prospects.

\subsection{Portfolio and Project Definition}
\label{subsec:portfolio}

A project \(i\in P\) is defined by its planned start \(s_i\), planned finish \(f_i\), planned duration \(D_i = f_i - s_i + 1\), and budget at completion \(\mathrm{BAC}_i\). The project is active only during its execution window \(\mathcal{A}_i = \{t : s_i \leq t \leq f_i\}\). At time \(t\) the set of active projects is \(P_t = \{i \in P : t \in \mathcal{A}_i\}\).

\subsection{Project Condition, Expenditure, and Cash Flows}
\label{subsec:state_cash}

The condition of project \(i\) at time \(t\) is described by the following set of variables:
\begin{equation}
\Omega_{i,t} = \bigl( p_{i,t},\ \mathrm{SPI}_{i,t},\ \mathrm{CPI}_{i,t},\ RD_{i,t},\ h_{i,t},\ \rho_{i,t} \bigr),
\end{equation}
whose meaning is given in \autoref{tab:state_vars}.

\subsubsection{Expenditure Dynamics}

Planned expenditure follows an S‑curve modelled by the regularised incomplete Beta function:
\begin{equation}
F_i(\tau) = I_{\tau}(\alpha_i,\beta_i),\quad \tau = (t - s_i)/D_i \in [0,1],
\end{equation}
with \(\alpha_i,\beta_i>0\) shaping the cost profile. Planned cumulative and period expenditures are \(\bar{E}_{i,t} = \mathrm{BAC}_i\,F_i(\tau_{i,t})\) and \(\bar{e}_{i,t} = \bar{E}_{i,t} - \bar{E}_{i,t-1}\). Actual expenditure \(e_{i,t}\) may differ from the plan due to performance variations or management actions. The aggregate planned outflow is \(\bar{E}^{P}_{t} = \sum_{i\in P_t} \bar{e}_{i,t}\).

\subsubsection{Milestone‑Based Revenue}

Each project has a deterministic contract revenue \(R_i = \mathrm{BAC}_i(1+\pi_i)\), received through a set of payment milestones \(K_i = \{0,1,\dots,N_i\}\). Milestone \(k\) carries a payment fraction \(q_{i,k} \ge 0\) with \(\sum_k q_{i,k}=1\) and a gross payment \(P_{i,k}^{\mathrm{gross}} = q_{i,k}R_i\).

Payments are triggered by progress. Let \(\chi_{i,k}\in[0,1]\) be the progress threshold for milestone \(k\). The planned achievement time is \(t_{i,k}^{\mathrm{plan}} = s_i + \chi_{i,k} D_i\). The actual achievement time depends on schedule performance:
\[
t_{i,k}^{\mathrm{act}} = s_i + \frac{\chi_{i,k} D_i}{\overline{\mathrm{SPI}}_{i,k}},
\]
where \(\overline{\mathrm{SPI}}_{i,k}\) is an effective schedule performance measure. A client‑side processing delay \(\delta_{i,k} \ge 0\) shifts the cash receipt to \(t_{i,k}^{\mathrm{rec}} = t_{i,k}^{\mathrm{act}} + \delta_{i,k}\). For the advance payment (\(k=0\)), we assume \(\delta_{i,0}=0\).

A retention rate \(r_i \in [0,1]\) is withheld from each payment as security. For non‑final milestones, the net receipt is \(P_{i,k}^{\mathrm{net}} = (1-r_i)P_{i,k}^{\mathrm{gross}}\). Accumulated retention is released at project completion (milestone \(N_i\)):
\[
P_{i,N_i}^{\mathrm{net}} = (1-r_i)P_{i,N_i}^{\mathrm{gross}} + \sum_{j=0}^{N_i-1} r_i P_{i,j}^{\mathrm{gross}}.
\]
Total lifecycle receipts satisfy \(\sum_k P_{i,k}^{\mathrm{net}} = R_i\).

Period revenue inflow from project \(i\) is \(Y_{i,t} = \sum_{k\in K_i} P_{i,k}^{\mathrm{net}} \,\mathbf{1}\{t_{i,k}^{\mathrm{rec}} = t\}\), and aggregate portfolio revenue is \(Y_t^P = \sum_{i\in P} Y_{i,t}\).

\subsubsection{Working Capital and Credit Limits}

Each project has a working capital account that evolves as
\begin{equation}
W_{i,t} = W_{i,t-1} + Y_{i,t} - e_{i,t} - c_{i,t}^{\mathrm{credit}},
\end{equation}
where \(c_{i,t}^{\mathrm{credit}}\) represents penalty costs from credit violations. A project‑specific credit limit \(\mathrm{CL}_i\) is set, proportional to \(\mathrm{BAC}_i\), and may be reduced by advance payments:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CL}_i = \beta_i \, \mathrm{BAC}_i \, \bigl(1 - \gamma_i A_i \bigr),
\end{equation}
with \(A_i = q_{i,0}\) and parameters \(\beta_i, \gamma_i\) reflecting contractual and financial risk. A violation occurs when negative working capital exceeds the limit,
\[
V_{i,t} = \max\{0,\, -W_{i,t} - \mathrm{CL}_i\},
\]
and triggers a penalty cost \(c_{i,t}^{\mathrm{credit}} = \rho_i V_{i,t}\) (or a more general convex function). The total portfolio credit‑violation cost is \(C_t^{\mathrm{credit}} = \sum_{i\in P_t} c_{i,t}^{\mathrm{credit}}\).

\subsubsection{Funding Balance}

The total budget available in period \(t\) comes from exogenous funds \(B^0_t\), revenue inflows, and external financing \(L_t\):
\[
B_t = B^0_t + Y_t^P + L_t.
\]
If required expenditure and intervention costs exceed \(B_t\), unmet funding appears:
\[
U_t = \max\Bigl\{0,\ \sum_{i\in P_t} e_{i,t} + C_t^{\mathrm{act}} - B_t\Bigr\},
\]
which can cause further delays or extra costs.

\subsection{Intervention Management}
\label{subsec:interventions}

Management can activate corrective interventions to improve schedule performance. Let \(u_{i,t} \in \{0,1\}\) indicate whether an intervention is applied to project \(i\) at time \(t\). The cost of an intervention is proportional to the schedule gap \(G_{i,t} = \max\{0,\,1 - \mathrm{SPI}_{i,t}\}\) and the project size:
\begin{equation}
C_{i,t}^{\mathrm{act}} = \kappa_i \, \mathrm{BAC}_i \, G_{i,t}, \quad \kappa_i \ge 0.
\end{equation}

During an intervention, the effective schedule performance improves to
\[
\mathrm{SPI}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{eff}} = \mathrm{SPI}_{i,t} + \eta_{i,t}^{\mathrm{SPI}} (1 - \mathrm{SPI}_{i,t});
\]
a similar enhancement \(\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{eff}} = \mathrm{CPI}_{i,t} (1 + \eta_{i,t}^{\mathrm{CPI}})\) may apply to cost performance. After the intervention ends, the improvement decays smoothly toward a long‑run equilibrium:
\[
\mathrm{SPI}_{i,t^{\mathrm{end}}_i+s} = \mathrm{SPI}_{i}^{\mathrm{eq}} + (1-\rho_i) \Delta^{\mathrm{peak}}_{i} \exp(-\lambda_i^{\mathrm{decay}} s),
\]
with parameters governing the peak improvement \(\Delta^{\mathrm{peak}}_{i}\), the retention factor \(\rho_i\), and the decay rate \(\lambda_i^{\mathrm{decay}}\).

The expected benefit of an intervention consists of delay avoidance, cost‑overrun avoidance, and opportunity gains:
\begin{align}
B_{i,t}^{\mathrm{LD}}   &= \lambda_i \, \mathrm{BAC}_i \, \mathbb{E}\!\bigl[ \Delta D^{\mathrm{avoided}}_{i,t} \bigr], \label{eq:benefit_delay} \\
B_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cost}}  &= \mathrm{BAC}_i \, \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \frac{1}{\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}} - \frac{1}{\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}^{\mathrm{eff}}} \right], \\
B_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opp}}   &= r_i V_i \, \mathbb{E}\!\bigl[ \Delta D^{\mathrm{avoided}}_{i,t} \bigr], \notag
\end{align}
where \(\lambda_i\) is a delay penalty, \(V_i\) the project value rate, and \(\Delta D^{\mathrm{avoided}}_{i,t}\) the reduction in expected duration. Total expected benefit is \(\mathbb{E}[B_{i,t}^{\mathrm{tot}}] = B_{i,t}^{\mathrm{LD}} + B_{i,t}^{\mathrm{cost}} + B_{i,t}^{\mathrm{opp}}\).

Portfolio‑level intervention expenditure is \(C_t^{\mathrm{act}} = \sum_{i\in P_t} u_{i,t} C_{i,t}^{\mathrm{act}}\), and must respect an intervention budget \(B_t^{\mathrm{act}}\). A benefit–cost ratio \(\mathrm{BCR}_t^P\) may be computed when \(C_t^{\mathrm{act}} > 0\) as a diagnostic.

\subsection{Duration and Performance Uncertainty}
\label{subsec:uncertainty}

Project duration is uncertain. The baseline duration \(D_i^{\mathrm{baseline}}\) follows a distribution \(\mathcal{D}_i\); the realised duration can be written as \(D_i = D_i^{\mathrm{baseline}} M_i\), where \(M_i \ge 0\) captures disruption or recovery effects. The performance indices \(\mathrm{SPI}_{i,t}\) and \(\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}\) evolve stochastically, driven by the project condition and external shocks. No specific parametric form is imposed; the formulation remains distribution‑free.

\subsection{Sequential Decision Problem and Objective}
\label{subsec:decision_objective}

At each period \(t\), the decision maker observes the project conditions \(\mathbf{\Omega}_t\), the available budget \(B_t\), and the intervention budget \(B_t^{\mathrm{act}}\), then chooses
\[
a_t = \bigl( \{e_{i,t}\}_{i\in P_t},\ \{u_{i,t}\}_{i\in P_t} \bigr),
\]
subject to the funding constraint \(\sum_{i\in P_t} e_{i,t} + C_t^{\mathrm{act}} \le B_t\), the intervention budget constraint \(\sum_{i\in P_t} u_{i,t} C_{i,t}^{\mathrm{act}} \le B_t^{\mathrm{act}}\), and any project‑specific operational limits.

The problem is a multi‑stage stochastic optimisation: the decision maker looks for a sequence of non‑anticipative decisions \(\{a_t\}_{t=1}^{H}\) that maximises the expected value of a composite portfolio performance measure. Formally,
\begin{equation}
\max\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[ \sum_{t=1}^{H} \Bigl( \phi_V V_t - \phi_U U_t - \phi_D D_t - \phi_{\!C} C_t^{\mathrm{act}} - \phi_{\!Cr} C_t^{\mathrm{credit}} - \phi_{\!B} \sum_{i\in P_t} \frac{e_{i,t}}{\bar{e}} \,\mathbf{1}\{\hat{\mu}_{i,t} < \underline{\mu}_i\} \Bigr) \right],
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
subject to the system dynamics defined in Sections~\ref{subsec:state_cash}--\ref{subsec:interventions} and the information constraint (decisions at \(t\) depend only on observations up to and including \(t\)). In~(\ref{eq:objective}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \(V_t\) is realised project value or benefit,
\item \(U_t\) is unmet funding,
\item \(D_t\) is aggregate delay exposure,
\item \(C_t^{\mathrm{act}}\) and \(C_t^{\mathrm{credit}}\) are intervention and credit‑violation costs,
\item \(\hat{\mu}_{i,t}\) is an estimate of project \(i\)'s remaining profitability (e.g., expected continuation margin),
\item \(\underline{\mu}_i\) is a management‑defined threshold below which further spending is deemed uneconomical,
\item and the non‑negative coefficients \(\phi_{(\cdot)}\) represent the relative importance of each term.
\end{itemize}

The last term penalises spending on projects whose expected continuation value has fallen below the critical threshold, thereby encouraging the rational suspension of unprofitable work. The exact composition of the performance measure, the shape of the penalty for poor‑prospect projects, and the weights \(\phi_{(\cdot)}\) are management inputs that reflect the organisation’s risk appetite, financial strategy, and contractual environment. The formulation thus provides a flexible template that can be tailored to different contexts, not a single fixed objective.

\subsection{Assumptions, Scope, and Limitations}
\label{subsec:assumptions}

\begin{itemize}
    \item The project portfolio is fixed; project admission or selection decisions are outside the scope.
    \item Expenditure profiles are represented conceptually by S‑curves; exact shapes may vary by project.
    \item Duration, cost, and performance uncertainties are kept distribution‑free.
    \item Intervention effects decay smoothly after the intervention ends. In reality, performance may change abruptly due to external shocks.
    \item All retention is released at project completion. A separate defects liability period is not modelled, as it lies beyond the active planning horizon.
    \item Credit limits are soft: violations incur penalty costs instead of forcing project termination. The organisation is assumed to be a rational, profit‑conscious contractor that may suspend a project when its expected net contribution turns negative.
    \item The formulation is solution‑neutral and can be addressed by various sequential decision‑making methods (e.g., stochastic dynamic programming, simulation‑based optimisation, or reinforcement learning).
\end{itemize}
