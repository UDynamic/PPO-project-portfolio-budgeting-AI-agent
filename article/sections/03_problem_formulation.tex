\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

\subsection{Management Problem and Portfolio Definition}
\label{subsec:management_problem}

We consider a multi-period budget allocation and operational control problem for a fixed portfolio of contracted projects \cite{martinsuo2013project}. The portfolio is defined as the aggregation of a finite set of projects,
\[
P=\{1,2,\ldots,n\},
\]
where each project \(i\in P\) represents a contractual undertaking with its own budget, planned schedule, expenditure profile, payment structure, and performance state. The portfolio is not a decision variable in the sense of project selection \cite{archer1999integrated,cooper2001portfolio}. Instead, it is the set of ongoing and committed projects whose execution must be managed jointly under limited financial and operational resources.

The planning horizon is indexed by discrete periods \(t=1,\ldots,H\). At each period, management observes the current portfolio condition, allocates financial resources across active projects, and may apply corrective interventions to improve schedule or cost performance \cite{lambrechts2008proactive,vanhoucke2012integrated}. Projects consume resources over time, generate cash inflows through milestone-based payments, and may deviate from their contractual plans because of schedule delays, cost overruns, funding shortages, payment delays, contractor performance uncertainty, or operational disruptions \cite{flyvbjerg2003underestimating,merrow2011industrial}.

The central management problem is to determine a sequential policy for budget allocation and intervention decisions that balances multiple objectives: completing projects close to their planned finish dates, preserving profitability, avoiding excessive borrowing, reducing delay penalties, limiting wasteful expenditure on economically unattractive projects, and maintaining acceptable portfolio-level financial health \cite{liesio2007portfolio}. Different organizations may prioritize these goals differently.

A key feature of the problem is that budget allocation and intervention decisions do not produce known deterministic performance outcomes. After management allocates resources and applies an intervention decision in period \(t\), the contractor's realized response is uncertain and is only observed at the next reporting epoch through project-control information such as realized progress, earned value, schedule performance, cost performance, and updated remaining-duration estimates \cite{anbari2003earned,fleming2016earned}. This makes the problem naturally sequential and feedback-driven \cite{puterman1994markov}.

\subsection{Contractual Project Attributes}
\label{subsec:contractual_attributes}

Each project \(i\in P\) is described by contractual and planned attributes known at the beginning of the planning process. Let \(s_i\) and \(f_i\) denote the planned start and planned finish periods, respectively. The planned duration is
\[
D_i^{\mathrm{plan}} = f_i-s_i+1,
\]
and the contractual planned execution window is \(\mathcal{A}_i^{\mathrm{plan}}=\{t:s_i\leq t\leq f_i\}\).

The budget at completion of project \(i\) is denoted by \(\mathrm{BAC}_i\). This value represents the planned cost basis of the project and is used to define planned expenditure, contract revenue, intervention cost scaling, and credit exposure. The contract revenue is \(CR_i=\mathrm{BAC}_i(1+\pi_i)\), where \(\pi_i\) is the contractual profit margin.

\subsection{Planned Expenditure and Contractual S-Curve}
\label{subsec:planned_expenditure}

The planned cumulative expenditure of each project follows a normalized S-curve \cite{barraza2004probabilistic}. The normalized planned time is \(\tau_{i,t}=(t-s_i+1)/D_i^{\mathrm{plan}}\) for \(t\in \mathcal{A}_i^{\mathrm{plan}}\), with truncation \(\tau_{i,t}^{c}=\min\{1,\max\{0,\tau_{i,t}\}\}\).

The planned cumulative expenditure fraction is modeled as \(F_i(\tau)=I_{\tau}(a_i,b_i)\), where \(I_{\tau}(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the regularized incomplete Beta function and \(a_i,b_i>0\) are shape parameters. The planned cumulative expenditure is \(\bar{E}_{i,t}=\mathrm{BAC}_i F_i(\tau_{i,t}^{c})\), and the planned period expenditure is \(\bar{e}_{i,t}=\bar{E}_{i,t}-\bar{E}_{i,t-1}\). This S-curve represents the contractual baseline spending trajectory used for measuring planned progress, schedule deviation, and performance indices \cite{anbari2003earned}.

\subsection{State, Decisions, and Dynamics}
\label{subsec:state_decisions}

\subsubsection{Project State Variables}
At each period \(t\), project \(i\) is described by a state vector including realized physical progress \(y_{i,t}\in[0,1]\), actual cumulative expenditure \(E_{i,t}\geq 0\), schedule performance index \(\mathrm{SPI}_{i,t}\), cost performance index \(\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}\), and remaining-duration estimate \(RD_{i,t}\) \cite{anbari2003earned,lipke2009prediction}. A project is active if \(y_{i,t}<1\) and inactive otherwise. The portfolio state at period \(t\) is the collection of all project states, financial positions, and credit levels.

\subsubsection{Decision Variables}
Management decisions at period \(t\) consist of budget allocation \(b_{i,t}\geq 0\) and intervention decision \(a_{i,t}\in\mathcal{A}_i\) for each active project, where \(\mathcal{A}_i\) is a discrete set of corrective actions including no intervention, schedule acceleration, resource augmentation, quality improvement, and scope adjustment \cite{lambrechts2008proactive}. The portfolio-level decision is \(\mathbf{u}_t=\{(b_{i,t},a_{i,t}):i\in P,y_{i,t-1}<1\}\).

\subsubsection{State Transition and Uncertainty}
After decisions are applied in period \(t\), the environment generates next-period project states through uncertain contractor response. The transition is represented implicitly through a project-control model \(\mathcal{M}\) that captures uncertainty in contractor performance, intervention effectiveness, and operational disruptions. The model does not assume known transition probabilities; instead, state evolution is either simulated using calibrated stochastic models or learned from data using reinforcement learning techniques \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}. This differs from resource-constrained project scheduling models \cite{ballesteros2015resource,kolisch1997psplib}, where task durations follow known distributions.

\subsection{Financial Accounting and Cash Flow}
\label{subsec:financial_accounting}

\subsubsection{Revenue Recognition and Milestone Payments}
Contract revenue is recognized through milestone-based progress payments \cite{christensen1995accounting}. For each project \(i\), milestones \(\{m_1,\ldots,m_K\}\) are defined as progress thresholds with payment fractions \(\{\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_K\}\) such that \(\sum_{k=1}^K\rho_k=1\). A retention fraction \(\lambda\in(0,1)\) is withheld from interim payments and released at completion.

When project \(i\) crosses milestone \(k\) in period \(t\), the recognized payment is
\[
P_{i,t}^k
=
\begin{cases}
\rho_k CR_i(1-\lambda), & k<K,\\
\rho_K CR_i + \lambda CR_i, & k=K.
\end{cases}
\]

\subsubsection{Operating Value and Portfolio Cash Flow}
The period operating value for project \(i\) is \(V_{i,t}=P_{i,t}-e_{i,t}\), where \(P_{i,t}=\sum_{k=1}^K P_{i,t}^k\) is the total recognized payment and \(e_{i,t}\) is actual period expenditure. Portfolio operating value is \(V_t=\sum_{i\in P}V_{i,t}\). Net cash flow after intervention costs is \(\Phi_t=V_t-C_t^{\mathrm{act}}\), where \(C_t^{\mathrm{act}}=\sum_{i\in P}c_i(a_{i,t})\).

\subsubsection{Credit Dynamics and Financing}
When net cash flow is negative, the portfolio requires external financing. Outstanding credit evolves as \(L_t=\max\{0,L_{t-1}-\Phi_t\}\). A credit limit \(\bar{L}\) is imposed as a soft constraint, with violations penalized through \(C_t^{\mathrm{credit}}=\phi(L_t-\bar{L})\), where \(\phi(\cdot)\) is convex increasing with \(\phi(x)=0\) for \(x\leq 0\). Financing cost is \(C_t^{\mathrm{fin}}=r L_t\), where \(r\) is the period interest rate.

\subsection{Performance Metrics and Penalties}
\label{subsec:performance_metrics}

\subsubsection{Delay Exposure}
For project \(i\), delay in period \(t\) is \(d_{i,t}=\max\{0,t-f_i\}\) if \(y_{i,t}<1\), representing periods the project has overrun its planned finish while incomplete. Aggregate delay exposure is \(D_t=\sum_{i\in P}w_i d_{i,t}\), where \(w_i\) reflects project importance.

\subsubsection{Liquidated Damages}
Upon completion, if project \(i\) finishes at period \(T_i>f_i\), liquidated damages are \(C_i^{\mathrm{LD}}=\psi_i(T_i-f_i)\), where \(\psi_i(\cdot)\) is a project-specific penalty function, typically linear or convex increasing in delay.

\subsubsection{Continuation Margin}
The estimated continuation margin \(\hat{\mu}_{i,t}\) represents expected profitability of continuing project \(i\), computed as the ratio of expected remaining revenue to expected remaining cost. Projects with \(\hat{\mu}_{i,t}<\underline{\mu}_i\) (a management-defined minimum acceptable margin) are considered economically unattractive and incur a penalty discouraging further investment.

\subsection{Optimization Objective}
\label{subsec:optimization_objective}

The objective is to find a policy \(\pi:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{U}\) mapping portfolio state \(\mathbf{s}_t\in\mathcal{S}\) to decisions \(\mathbf{u}_t\in\mathcal{U}\), maximizing expected cumulative reward \cite{puterman1994markov,sutton2018reinforcement}:
\[
\max_{\pi}\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=1}^H\gamma^{t-1}R_t\right],
\]
where \(\gamma\in(0,1]\) is a discount factor, and the period reward is:
\begin{align}
R_t &= V_t - \alpha_D D_t - C_t^{\mathrm{act}} - C_t^{\mathrm{fin}} - C_t^{\mathrm{credit}} \nonumber\\
&\quad - \sum_{i\in P:y_{i,t}=1}\left[C_i^{\mathrm{LD}} + \beta\max\{0,\underline{\mu}_i-\hat{\mu}_{i,t}\}\right].
\label{eq:period_reward}
\end{align}

Here, \(\alpha_D\geq 0\) is the delay penalty weight and \(\beta\geq 0\) controls the penalty for investing in projects with weak continuation margins. The expectation is taken over the trajectory distribution induced by policy \(\pi\) and the project-control environment \(\mathcal{M}\). In a reinforcement-learning implementation, this expectation is approximated through sampled trajectories \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.

\subsection{Constraints and Feasibility}
\label{subsec:constraints}

Total allocated budget in each period must satisfy \(\sum_{i\in P:y_{i,t-1}<1}b_{i,t}\leq B_t+L_t\), where \(B_t\) is available internal funds and \(L_t\) is accessible credit. Certain interventions may be restricted by project phase, contractual terms, or resource availability, encoded in the action space \(\mathcal{A}_i\) and enforced through action masking. A project completes when \(y_{i,t}\geq 1\), after which no further budget allocation or intervention is permitted.

\subsection{Modeling Assumptions and Scope}
\label{subsec:assumptions}

The formulation embeds several structural assumptions that define its scope and distinguish it from related models:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The portfolio is fixed; project selection is not modeled \cite{archer1999integrated,cooper2001portfolio,liesio2007portfolio}.
    \item Projects are modeled as independent execution units; technical dependencies and resource sharing are not explicitly represented \cite{ballesteros2015resource}.
    \item Budget allocation and interventions influence contractor performance through uncertain mechanisms, with realized effects observed only at the next reporting epoch \cite{lambrechts2008proactive}.
    \item Performance indices, progress observations, and remaining-duration estimates are treated as project-control information rather than outputs of a fixed deterministic production law \cite{anbari2003earned,fleming2016earned}.
    \item Planned project finish dates are fixed contractually, but actual project completion times are endogenous outcomes determined by contractor performance and managerial decisions \cite{flyvbjerg2003underestimating}.
    \item Duration, cost, payment, contractor-performance, and intervention-effectiveness uncertainties are kept distribution-free at the formulation level.
    \item The formulation does not require known transition probabilities or known future uncertainty distributions in advance; dynamics are learned or simulated \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.
    \item Milestone payments are triggered by realized progress and recognized in discrete periods \cite{christensen1995accounting}.
    \item Retention is withheld from interim progress payments and fully released with the final milestone at project completion.
    \item A separate defects liability period is not modeled because it lies beyond the active execution-focused planning horizon.
    \item Funding shortages are feasible through credit or external financing, but they generate financing costs and possible credit-limit penalties.
    \item Credit limits are soft constraints; violations are allowed but penalized through credit-cost functions.
    \item Corrective interventions may improve observed performance indicators, but their benefits are primarily captured through improved project outcomes rather than added as separate reward terms \cite{lambrechts2008proactive,vanhoucke2012integrated}.
    \item The formulation defines the decision, accounting, and performance-evaluation structure. Implementation through simulation, stochastic optimization, approximate dynamic programming, or reinforcement learning requires an empirical, scenario-based, simulator-driven, or learned mechanism for generating next-period project states \cite{sutton2018reinforcement}.
\end{itemize}

This formulation provides a novel integration of portfolio-level budget allocation, project-level earned-value dynamics, milestone-based revenue recognition, credit-constrained financing, and sequential decision-making under uncertainty. Unlike traditional project portfolio selection models \cite{archer1999integrated,liesio2007portfolio,gutjahr2013project}, the focus is on operational control of committed projects rather than ex-ante selection. The formulation extends earned-value management frameworks \cite{anbari2003earned,fleming2016earned,lipke2009prediction} by embedding them within a sequential decision framework with financial accounting, credit dynamics, and multi-objective performance evaluation, while avoiding the strong distributional assumptions typical in resource-constrained project scheduling \cite{ballesteros2015resource,golenko1997stochastic}.
