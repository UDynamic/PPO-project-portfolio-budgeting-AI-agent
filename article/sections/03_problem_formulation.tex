
This formulation provides a novel representation of project portfolio budget allocation that integrates mandatory minimum funding requirements, soft budget constraints with endogenous credit dynamics, performance-triggered corrective interventions, and milestone-based revenue recognition within a unified stochastic, multi-period optimization framework. Unlike traditional project portfolio selection models \cite{archer1999integrated,liesio2007portfolio,gutjahr2013project}, the focus is on operational control of committed projects rather than ex-ante selection. The formulation extends earned-value management frameworks \cite{anbari2003earned,fleming2016earned,lipke2008prediction} by embedding them within a sequential decision framework with explicit financial accounting, soft budget constraints, endogenous intervention mechanisms, and multi-objective performance evaluation, while remaining solution-agnostic and compatible with diverse solution methodologies including reinforcement learning, approximate dynamic programming, or scenario-based stochastic optimization.

\subsection{Management Problem and Portfolio Definition}
\label{subsec:management_problem}

We consider a stochastic, multi-period budget allocation problem for a fixed portfolio of contracted projects \cite{martinsuo2013project}. The portfolio is defined as the aggregation of a finite set of projects,
\[
\mathcal{P}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\},
\]
where each project \(i\in \mathcal{P}\) represents a contractual undertaking with its own budget, planned schedule, expenditure profile, payment structure, and performance state. The portfolio is not a decision variable in the sense of project selection \cite{archer1999integrated,cooper2001portfolio}. Instead, it is the set of ongoing and committed projects whose execution must be managed jointly under limited financial and operational resources.

The planning horizon is indexed by discrete periods \(t\in\mathcal{T}=\{1,\ldots,H\}\). At each period, the decision-maker observes the current portfolio condition and allocates financial resources across active projects \cite{lambrechts2008proactive,vanhoucke2012integrated}. Projects consume resources over time, generate cash inflows through milestone-based payments, and may deviate from their contractual plans because of schedule delays, cost overruns, funding shortages, payment delays, contractor performance uncertainty, or operational disruptions \cite{flyvbjerg2003underestimating,merrow2011industrial}.

The central management problem is to determine a sequential allocation policy that balances multiple objectives: completing projects close to their planned finish dates, preserving profitability, avoiding excessive borrowing, reducing delay penalties, limiting wasteful expenditure on economically unattractive projects, and maintaining acceptable portfolio-level financial health \cite{liesio2007portfolio}. Different organizations may prioritize these goals differently.

A key feature of the problem is that budget allocation decisions do not produce known deterministic performance outcomes. After resources are allocated in period \(t\), the contractor's realized response is uncertain and is only observed at the next reporting epoch through project-control information such as realized progress, earned value, schedule performance, cost performance, and updated remaining-duration estimates \cite{anbari2003earned,fleming2016earned}. This makes the problem naturally sequential and feedback-driven under uncertainty.

\subsection{Contractual Project Attributes}
\label{subsec:contractual_attributes}

Each project \(i\in \mathcal{P}\) is described by contractual and planned attributes known at the beginning of the planning process. Let \(s_i\) and \(f_i\) denote the planned start and planned finish periods, respectively. The planned duration is
\[
D_i^{\mathrm{plan}} = f_i-s_i+1,
\]
and the contractual planned execution window is \(\mathcal{A}_i^{\mathrm{plan}}=\{t:s_i\leq t\leq f_i\}\).

The budget at completion of project \(i\) is denoted by \(\mathrm{BAC}_i\). This value represents the planned cost basis of the project and is used to define planned expenditure, contract revenue, and credit exposure. The contract revenue is \(CR_i=\mathrm{BAC}_i(1+\pi_i)\), where \(\pi_i\) is the contractual profit margin.

\subsection{Planned Expenditure and Contractual S-Curve}
\label{subsec:planned_expenditure}

The planned cumulative expenditure of each project follows a normalized S-curve \cite{barraza2004probabilistic}. The normalized planned time is \(\tau_{i,t}=(t-s_i+1)/D_i^{\mathrm{plan}}\) for \(t\in \mathcal{A}_i^{\mathrm{plan}}\), with truncation \(\tau_{i,t}^{c}=\min\{1,\max\{0,\tau_{i,t}\}\}\).

The planned cumulative expenditure fraction is modeled as \(F_i(\tau)=I_{\tau}(a_i,b_i)\), where \(I_{\tau}(\cdot,\cdot)\) is the regularized incomplete Beta function and \(a_i,b_i>0\) are shape parameters. The planned cumulative expenditure is \(\bar{E}_{i,t}=\mathrm{BAC}_i F_i(\tau_{i,t}^{c})\), and the planned period expenditure is \(\bar{e}_{i,t}=\bar{E}_{i,t}-\bar{E}_{i,t-1}\). This S-curve represents the contractual baseline spending trajectory used for measuring planned progress, schedule deviation, and performance indices \cite{anbari2003earned}.

\subsection{Decision Variables and Constraints}
\label{subsec:decisions_constraints}

The decision-maker's choice at each period \(t\) is the allocation of budget to each active project. Let \(x_{i,t}\geq 0\) denote the budget allocated to project \(i\) at time \(t\). A project is considered active if its cumulative progress \(P_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{actual}}<1\). The allocation decision is constrained by project-level mandatory minimum requirements and portfolio-level budget availability.

\subsubsection{Mandatory Minimum Allocation Constraint}

Each project \(i\) at time \(t\) has a minimum mandatory allocation \(m_{i,t}\geq 0\) reflecting contractual obligations, debt-service requirements, or minimum execution commitments \cite{AlSedairy2001role,PMI2017pmbok}. The allocation must satisfy
\[
x_{i,t} \geq m_{i,t}, \quad \forall i\in\mathcal{P}, \, t\in\mathcal{T}.
\]
This constraint ensures that projects receive at least the minimum required funding to meet operational, contractual, or financial obligations in each period. If a project uses credit or triggers corrective intervention mechanisms, future mandatory allocations may increase to reflect additional cashflow requirements.

\subsubsection{Soft Budget Constraint and Credit Mechanism}

The portfolio has an available internal budget \(B_t^{\mathrm{avail}}\) at each period \(t\). If the total allocation across active projects exceeds the available internal budget, the excess is financed through external credit. Let
\[
c_t = \max\left(0, \sum_{i\in\mathcal{P}:P_{i,t-1}^{\mathrm{actual}}<1} x_{i,t} - B_t^{\mathrm{avail}}\right)
\]
denote the credit used at time \(t\). Credit is allowed but creates interest-bearing debt that increases future cashflow obligations \cite{AlJibouri2002monitoring,Flyvbjerg2014megaprojects}.

Outstanding debt evolves according to
\[
D_{t+1} = (1+r_t)D_t + c_t - q_t,
\]
where \(D_t\) is the debt at the start of period \(t\), \(r_t\geq 0\) is the interest rate, and \(q_t\geq 0\) is the repayment made in period \(t\). If repayment is not separately modeled, the debt accumulates as \(D_{t+1}=(1+r_t)D_t+c_t\). The credit mechanism is thus modeled as a soft budget constraint: allocations exceeding internal funds are feasible but impose financial penalties through interest charges.

\subsection{Project State and Performance Dynamics}
\label{subsec:state_dynamics}

\subsubsection{Project State Variables}

At each period \(t\), project \(i\) is described by a state vector including planned cumulative progress \(P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{plan}}\in[0,1]\), actual cumulative progress \(P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}}\in[0,1]\), actual cumulative expenditure \(E_{i,t}\geq 0\), schedule performance index \(\mathrm{SPI}_{i,t}\), cost performance index \(\mathrm{CPI}_{i,t}\), and remaining-duration estimate \(RD_{i,t}\) \cite{anbari2003earned,lipke2008prediction}. The performance gap is defined as
\[
\Delta_{i,t} = P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{plan}} - P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}}.
\]

\subsubsection{Performance-Triggered Corrective Intervention Mechanism}

Following earned-value management practice \cite{fleming2016earned,PMI2017pmbok}, each project is continuously monitored by the owner's representative or project management consultant (PMC). When the performance gap exceeds a contractually defined threshold \(\tau_i\), a corrective intervention is automatically initiated \cite{AlSedairy2001role,Ijaola2013recovery}. The intervention indicator is defined as
\[
I_{i,t} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \Delta_{i,t} > \tau_i, \\
0, & \Delta_{i,t} \leq \tau_i.
\end{cases}
\]

The intervention is not a decision variable. It is an endogenous, state-dependent corrective mechanism representing contractual and governance-driven responses such as recovery planning, escalation, closer monitoring, or contractor corrective action \cite{Hallgren2009deviations,Love2011praxis}. The intervention can be activated only if sufficient budget has been allocated to cover the associated management effort and potential acceleration costs. If \(x_{i,t}\) falls below the threshold necessary to support intervention activities, the intervention may fail to activate even when \(\Delta_{i,t}>\tau_i\).

\subsubsection{Effect of Intervention on Future Performance}

When intervention is triggered, it affects future project performance through two channels: increased near-term cashflow requirements and improved budget-to-progress conversion efficiency \cite{Williams2009essential}. Let \(\alpha_{i,t}\geq 0\) denote the baseline efficiency with which allocated budget translates into progress. The intervention-adjusted efficiency is
\[
\tilde{\alpha}_{i,t+1} = \alpha_{i,t+1}(1+\kappa_{i,t}I_{i,t}),
\]
where \(\kappa_{i,t}\geq 0\) is the intervention effectiveness parameter. This parameter may be deterministic or stochastic, reflecting the uncertain success of recovery plans observed in practice \cite{Ijaola2013recovery,Love2011praxis}.

The actual progress at \(t+1\) evolves as
\[
P_{i,t+1}^{\mathrm{actual}} = P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}} + \tilde{\alpha}_{i,t} x_{i,t} + \varepsilon_{i,t},
\]
where \(\varepsilon_{i,t}\) is a stochastic disturbance capturing contractor performance uncertainty.

The intervention also increases the mandatory minimum allocation for subsequent periods to reflect additional cashflow obligations:
\[
m_{i,t+1} = m_{i,t+1}^{0} + \chi_i I_{i,t},
\]
where \(m_{i,t+1}^{0}\) is the baseline mandatory allocation and \(\chi_i\geq 0\) captures the additional required funding induced by the intervention.

The allocation decision \(x_{i,t}\) influences intervention only indirectly: by affecting actual progress \(P_{i,t}^{\mathrm{actual}}\), it influences the performance gap \(\Delta_{i,t}\), which in turn determines whether the intervention threshold is breached. The decision-maker can thus learn to allocate budget strategically to either avoid intervention costs or deliberately trigger interventions when the expected performance gain justifies the expenditure.

\subsection{Revenue Recognition and Milestone-Based Payments}
\label{subsec:revenue}

Revenue is recognized through contractual milestone payments tied to verified physical progress \cite{barraza2004probabilistic}. Each project \(i\) has a set of milestones \(\mathcal{M}_i=\{1,\ldots,M_i\}\). Milestone \(j\in\mathcal{M}_i\) is associated with a progress threshold \(\theta_{i,j}\in(0,1]\) and a payment fraction \(\phi_{i,j}>0\) such that \(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{M}_i}\phi_{i,j}=1\). When actual progress reaches \(\theta_{i,j}\), the milestone payment
\[
R_{i,j} = \phi_{i,j}CR_i(1-\rho_i)
\]
is received, where \(\rho_i\in[0,1)\) is the retention fraction. Retention is released upon project completion. Let \(V_t\) denote the total milestone-based revenue received in period \(t\).

\subsection{Objective Function}
\label{subsec:objective}

The objective is to maximize the expected cumulative discounted performance across the planning horizon. Let \(\gamma\in(0,1]\) denote the discount factor. The period performance metric aggregates milestone-based revenue, delay penalties, credit costs, and other portfolio-level costs:
\[
R_t = V_t - \alpha_D D_t - C_t^{\mathrm{credit}} - C_t^{\mathrm{delay}} - C_t^{\mathrm{other}},
\]
where \(\alpha_D\geq 0\) weights the portfolio delay (total periods all projects are behind schedule), \(C_t^{\mathrm{credit}}\) represents interest and financing costs, \(C_t^{\mathrm{delay}}\) captures contractual liquidated damages, and \(C_t^{\mathrm{other}}\) includes other penalties or costs.

The total objective is
\[
\max \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{H}\gamma^{t-1}R_t\right],
\]
where the expectation is taken over the stochastic evolution of project states, contractor responses, and intervention outcomes. The allocation policy must respect the mandatory minimum allocation constraints and the soft budget constraint with endogenous credit dynamics.

\subsection{Scope and Modeling Assumptions}
\label{subsec:scope}

The formulation is subject to the following scope definitions and modeling assumptions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The portfolio composition is fixed. Project selection and termination decisions are not modeled.
    \item All projects are assumed to have been properly contracted with defined scopes, budgets, schedules, and payment structures before the planning horizon begins.
    \item The formulation is solution-agnostic. It can be embedded within a stochastic optimization framework, approximate dynamic programming, or a sequential decision-making environment for solution via reinforcement learning or other methods.
    \item Progress is measured as cumulative physical completion and is normalized to \([0,1]\). Earned value is computed as the product of progress and budget at completion.
    \item Retention is withheld from interim progress payments and fully released with the final milestone at project completion.
    \item A separate defects liability period is not modeled because it lies beyond the active execution-focused planning horizon.
    \item Credit use is induced by allocation decisions exceeding available internal budget. Credit limits may be enforced through penalty terms in the objective function.
    \item Corrective interventions are automatically triggered by performance gaps exceeding contractually defined thresholds. The intervention's effectiveness is uncertain and may be represented through stochastic parameters or disturbance terms.
    \item Intervention-related costs are reflected through increased mandatory minimum allocations in subsequent periods and through the credit mechanism if additional budget must be borrowed.
    \item The formulation defines the decision structure, constraints, state dynamics, and performance evaluation. Numerical solution requires specification of the stochastic processes governing contractor response, intervention effectiveness, and project state evolution.
\end{itemize}
